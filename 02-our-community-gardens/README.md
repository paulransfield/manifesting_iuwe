## The federation proposition

IUWE proposes shared technical and content capability alongside local cultural, linguistic, and operational sovereignty. A community may draw from common assets and common infrastructure while setting its own schedules, language priorities, kaitiaki roles, learner relationships, and rules of participation.

This is the distinction between a **shared commons** and a centrally owned platform. The shared layer makes reuse possible; the local layer protects context, accountability, and the right to say yes, no, not yet, or not in this form.

## The federation is already visible

The federation is not only a future diagram. It has public doors: twelve `iho.` learning ecosystems, a 24/7 visual stream pattern, a rotating-cube low-bandwidth alternative, learning hubs, learner practice apps, word and card-flip games, community-care passports, and a koha gateway. The [federated ecosystem registry](./FEDERATED_ECOSYSTEMS.md) records the current public entry points, their basic roles, and the small link corrections needed to prevent broken routes from entering the living book.

## One garden, many gardeners

| Shared across the federation | Held locally by each community |
|---|---|
| Reusable software patterns and source code | Community identity and governance |
| Provenance and asset-lifecycle methods | Language, dialect, and cultural review |
| Learning-event templates | Timetable, timezone, and local delivery rhythm |
| Interoperable identifiers and evidence formats | Kaitiaki, learner relationships, and permissions |
| A route to mutual support and collective advantage | Decisions about appropriate use and local reciprocity |

The pattern can serve Māori, Pacific, Sami, Mossi, Cherokee, and other communities without claiming that their needs are identical. The technical pattern may travel; the community’s authority and living language must not be abstracted away.

## Governance as an operating capability

Governance is not a page added after launch. It is present in everyday system choices: who can create an asset, who can approve a word sense, who sees a learner record, how a concern is raised, and whether a disputed resource can be paused.

The architecture therefore treats community review, provenance, requests, change, incident, and retirement as first-class processes. A small community should be able to understand and teach these processes, not rely on an invisible vendor to interpret them.

## The 80:20 relationship

The project uses an 80:20 koha relationship as a proposed operating pattern: most of a delivery contribution returns to local community capacity, while a smaller portion keeps shared infrastructure, curation, provenance, and federation maintenance viable. The figures are a transparent design proposition, not a substitute for each community deciding what is fair.

> **Koha is neither a hidden platform fee nor a decorative word for a charge. It is a traceable relationship of contribution, care, and accountability.**

## The community council question

Each participating community will need its own answer to several practical questions:

| Question | Design requirement |
|---|---|
| Who may speak for the community in this system? | Record clear roles and delegated authority. |
| Who reviews language, cultural context, and images? | Support more than one kaitiaki and preserve decisions. |
| What may travel beyond the community? | Make release scope explicit and reversible. |
| What is owed to contributors and creators? | Link provenance, wishes, and reciprocity to the asset lifecycle. |
| How are problems heard and resolved? | Provide a community endpoint and a documented resolution path. |

## Next small proof

A federation is proven by a modest shared asset being scheduled differently, responsibly, and visibly in two communities—not by a large central launch.
